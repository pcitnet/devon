# Ghosts and Strange Tales of Colebrooke Parish: Fact, Folklore and Mystery

There is something about an old Devon lane after dark: the hedges close in, familiar buildings become silhouettes, and the smallest sound seems to travel a long way. In a parish of old houses, winding roads and inns that have watched generations come and go, it is hardly surprising that stories of strange encounters have gathered.

But what ghost stories have actually been **recorded** in Colebrooke parish? And how much can we say about where they came from?

Five intriguing accounts can be traced in collections of British hauntings. One concerns the New Inn at Coleford and appears in more than one published folklore collection. The others largely come from anonymous accounts. They are interesting as stories, but a record of a claimed haunting is **not evidence that a ghost exists** — or that the events told in the story happened as described.

## Sebastian, the New Inn's mysterious visitor

Of the parish's supernatural traditions, the best-established **as a published legend** belongs to the New Inn at Coleford. The figure said to linger there goes by the name **Sebastian**. In some tellings he is a monk or friar; another published catalogue allows the rather different possibility that he was a Cavalier.

The accounts do not even agree on how he died. One version involves the discovery of livestock thieves and a fatal attempt to silence him. Another tells of a clandestine romance followed by a drowning in a nearby stream. The contradictions are part of what makes the tradition worth examining: they show how different explanations can become attached to the same supposed presence.

A separate tale associated with the inn describes a woman-like appearance preceded by the smell of perfume. That is another **reported experience**, not an independently documented sighting. Nor has a historical record been identified proving that a monk named Sebastian was murdered or drowned at Coleford.

The New Inn itself is entirely real, of course. Historic England lists it as a **Grade II building**, describing the principal surviving fabric as eighteenth- and nineteenth-century, with later alterations. This does not establish the age of every part of the site, but it does remind us to separate the architectural record from romantic claims of an ancient haunting.

Exactly when the Sebastian legend began to circulate locally remains uncertain. The published versions preserve the tale, but do not establish its origins.

## The woman beside the fireplace at Coombe House

Further east in the parish, the setting changes from village inn to country house. A haunting account recorded tells of a family at **Coombe House** during the Second World War. A young woman in a long dress appeared near a bedroom fireplace, and the family later heard that subsequent occupants had experienced something similar.

This is a story with an evocative setting, but its limitations are important. No contemporary diary, letter or newspaper report has yet been produced to corroborate it.

Coombe House is certainly a historic building within **Colebrooke parish**, not a house in the centre of Coleford as the folklore catalogue's broad location label might suggest. Historic England records an eighteenth-century core with nineteenth-century alterations. Those architectural facts are verifiable; the reported figure by the fireplace remains an unverified recollection.

Other curious stories have been told about Coombe's buildings and grounds, including underground passages, but that is a separate subject with its own documentary history. It would be a mistake to use one as proof of the other.

## A Lady in White at the Coleford crossroads

Crossroads have long lent themselves to stories. They are places where travellers meet, choose a direction or disappear from view — ideal surroundings for a tale that leaves more questions than answers.

Another account describes a **Lady in White** said to appear at the crossroads in Coleford. The account speculates that she is searching for a sister who may have died during the nineteenth century.

Yet the report gives neither a named witness nor an identifiable nineteenth-century death to test against parish registers. The suggestion about the sister should therefore remain part of the *legend*, not be repeated as a fact about a real family. Even the date of the supposed apparition is unknown.

For now, what we have is a recorded ghost story attached to a recognisable village location. Whether older inhabitants remember a similar tale, and how far back the tradition goes, would be worthwhile questions for local oral history.

## Annie, the woman in the brown dress

The next report is less easily placed on a map, which is probably just as well: it concerns an **unidentified private residence** in Coleford.

An account in a folklore collection describes an elderly woman in a brown dress, called **Annie**, together with unexplained footsteps, moving possessions and doors opening or shutting. Its contributor also suggested that the activity was particularly noticeable in **May and September**.

There is no confirmed identification of Annie, no documented link with a previous occupant and no independent evidence for the reported disturbances. The anonymity of the address should be respected. A story of this kind is best preserved as a piece of contemporary local folklore, without trying to attach it to a particular household or person on guesswork.

## The figure who passed through a wall

Perhaps the most curious of the accounts concerns an **unnamed cottage in Coleford**. The story tells of a family living there during the **1980s**, when the house was said to have belonged to a miller.

In the story, a figure dressed in dusty white work clothes was seen moving through what appeared to be a solid wall. Later alterations supposedly revealed that the same position had once contained a doorway.

It is an arresting detail. An old blocked opening is something a building survey could, in principle, establish. But the report does not identify the cottage, provide a plan or name its witnesses. Until supporting evidence appears, **both the sighting and the discovery of the doorway remain claims within the submitted story**.

There is no good reason to guess which Coleford cottage it might be. Doing so could unfairly attach a supernatural story to someone's home.

## What these tales tell us — and what they do not

These accounts come from **two different sorts of evidence**. The New Inn's Sebastian appears in published folklore collections, while the other four largely rely on unnamed contributors' recollections or assertions. None of the reported supernatural experiences has been independently verified.

That does not make them worthless. Legends are part of social history: they reveal the kinds of stories people associate with particular buildings and places, and how a community imagines its past. But good parish history must make room for the words *said*, *reported* and *unconfirmed*.

There is also a geographical trap. Ghost directories sometimes group **Coombe House** under “Coleford”, whereas the house stands in the wider **parish of Colebrooke**. Our parish boundary matters just as much for folklore as it does for deeds, censuses and old maps.



*Research note: This article records and assesses published folklore. It does not claim that any apparition was real or that the dramatic events proposed in the legends have been historically established.
