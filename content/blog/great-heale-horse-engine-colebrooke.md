# When Horses Powered the Barn: Great Heale's Forgotten Horse Engine

Long before a tractor or an electric motor could be started with the turn of a key, a farm could put a horse to work without moving a cart or pulling a plough. The horse simply walked in a circle. Its steady movement turned a wheel, the wheel drove gears, and the gears supplied power to machinery inside a barn.

One of Colebrooke's most interesting reminders of that older farming technology was recorded at **Great Heale Farm**. The evidence is unusually precise: an official historic-building record describes a horse-engine house beside the barn, and a survey in **2000** found that a significant amount of its gearing remained.

## A different kind of engine house

The word *engine* can suggest steam, diesel or electricity. Here it meant something much simpler. A **horse engine**, sometimes called a *horse gin*, used the movement of a horse walking around a central mechanism to provide rotary power. Gears and a connecting shaft carried that power to a machine that could do a job which had previously demanded heavy manual labour.

The Great Heale installation was associated with a **threshing machine**. Threshing separates the grain from the harvested ears and stalks. For generations this had been done by hand with flails; powered machinery offered another way to tackle the work after harvest.

We do not know from the available Great Heale record how many horses were used there, what the machine's make was, or the year in which the equipment first operated. Those details should not be invented simply because other farms used similar arrangements.

## What the 2000 survey actually recorded

The **Devon Historic Environment Record**, entry **MDV80640**, places the horse-engine house on the **west side of the barn** at Great Heale. It dates the addition broadly to the **nineteenth century**.

The building was originally a light structure: a timber framework with open spaces between its posts, supporting a thatched roof. By the time the building was examined in 2000, the roof covering had been replaced with corrugated iron and other boarding or cladding had been introduced.

More remarkable was the machinery. The survey noted that **a considerable portion of the geared horse wheel was still present**. The connecting drive shaft was no longer operating, and the threshing machine once served inside the barn had gone. Nevertheless, enough was recorded to show the earlier arrangement had not vanished without a trace.

The record also cites a **2000 photograph of the geared horse wheel**, taken by P. Child and held among Devon's historic-building records. That photograph would be especially valuable for a future illustrated version of this article, if permission can be obtained.

## An older barn adapted for changing agriculture

Another heritage record, **MDV104622**, provides the architectural setting. The corn barn at the western end of Great Heale's farmyard appears to date from the **eighteenth century**. It was probably once detached from the farmhouse before subsequent building work linked the ranges around the yard.

The horse-engine house came later. Its addition illustrates how older farm buildings were adapted to new methods rather than necessarily being demolished and replaced. A barn built for storing and handling crops could acquire powered equipment as farming practice changed.

A separate correspondence record from 2000 discusses proposals to refurbish the engine house, including the possibility of bringing back a thatched roof. **A proposal is not proof that the work was carried out**, and the publicly accessible record does not establish the structure's condition in 2026.

## Why the surviving gearing mattered

Historic England's guidance on traditional farm buildings explains why horse-engine houses deserve attention. Farm machinery changed, was dismantled, or was removed when no longer required; even where the small adjoining building remained, its working equipment could disappear.

That is what made the Great Heale observation particularly notable. The existence of a former wheelhouse is interesting. A wheelhouse documented with much of its **gear mechanism** still in place is more revealing, because it shows how energy was transferred from an animal to a machine.

The record gives us a rare glimpse of the working farmyard, not merely of the houses and families associated with it.

## Was Great Heale the only such farm in the parish?

No. Devon's Historic Environment Record separately identifies a **former horse-engine house at Whelmstone Barton**, attached to the north-west side of a barn. A detailed building survey associates it with alterations made around the late eighteenth or early nineteenth century. Unlike the Great Heale record, the Whelmstone entry describes a **former** building, not a geared wheel documented in position in 2000.

The two places should not be confused. Together, however, they show that horse-powered farm machinery was not an isolated idea in this part of Colebrooke.

## A piece of Colebrooke's working past

The history of a parish is found not only in deeds, censuses, churches and notable houses, but also in the devices that made everyday work possible. At Great Heale, the nineteenth-century decision to attach a horse engine to an older barn was part of that larger change.

**What can we say for certain?** In 2000 an observer documented a nineteenth-century horse-engine house at Great Heale and substantial remnants of its geared wheel. **What remains to be established?** Whether the gearing survives today, when the machinery last operated, and whether photographs or memories of its use can be found.
